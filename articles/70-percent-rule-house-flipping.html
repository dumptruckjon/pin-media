The 70 Percent Rule in House Flipping: What It Hides

The 70 percent rule in house flipping sounds like a complete pricing system: offer 70% of the after repair value minus repairs, done. Then the flip closes and the profit leaks out through holding costs, selling costs, and lines the rule never asked about. It's a decent ten-second screen that got promoted to law, and treating it like law is how first flips break even.

Where the rule comes from

Nobody derived it from your deal. The story investors usually tell traces the rule to hard money lenders, who would lend up to about 70% of a property's after repair value. Buy under that line and the loan could carry the whole project, with a 30% cushion under the lender. The band was sized to protect the lender in a foreclosure, not to pay you. Then it got repeated in seminars and YouTube videos until it sounded like physics. It's a lender-era screen, not a law, and it never itemizes a single cost.

The formula, run on a real deal

The rule in one line:


Max offer = (ARV x 0.70) - repair costs

MAO is the shorthand: maximum allowable offer. Here's a full deal run both ways.

The house should sell for $280,000 renovated. It needs $45,000 of work: roof, HVAC, kitchen, bathrooms, flooring, paint. Closing costs run $3,500. Holding costs, the taxes, insurance, and utilities you pay while you own it, come to $7,500 over six months. Selling costs at 8% of the resale are $22,400. You want $30,000 of profit. This is a cash purchase; borrow and the loan's interest and points join the stack.


After repair value (ARV)                 $280,000
Repairs                                   $45,000
Closing costs                              $3,500
Holding costs, 6 months                    $7,500
Selling costs, 8% of $280,000             $22,400
Profit you want                           $30,000

70% rule:
  $280,000 x 0.70                        $196,000
  minus $45,000 repairs                  $151,000

Full cost stack:
  $280,000 - $45,000 - $3,500 - $7,500
  - $22,400 - $30,000                    $171,600

Two ceilings, $20,600 apart. The full stack says $171,600 is the most this house is worth to you, with every cost and your profit already inside. The 70% rule says $151,000 and can't tell you why.

Now say the seller asks $160,000. That's $9,000 past the rule's ceiling and $11,600 under the honest one. The rule says walk. The stack says run it at the ask:


Costs besides purchase:
  $45,000 + $3,500 + $7,500 + $22,400     $78,400
Profit: $280,000 - $160,000 - $78,400     $41,600
Cash in: $160,000 + $45,000 + $3,500
  + $7,500                               $216,000
Cash-on-cash: $41,600 / $216,000           19.26%

Selling costs skip the cash-in line because they come out of the sale proceeds, not your pocket. So: $41,600 of profit on $216,000 of cash in six months, a 19.26% cash-on-cash return. Most flippers take that deal all day. The 70% rule threw it in the trash.

What the rule hides

The 30% the rule holds back has to cover everything that isn't purchase or repairs: closing, holding, selling, financing if you borrow, and your entire profit. It just never shows you the split.

On this deal the reserve is 30% of $280,000, or $84,000. What needs covering: $3,500 closing plus $7,500 holding plus $22,400 selling is $33,400, and with the $30,000 profit target that's $63,400. The leftover is $20,600, exactly the gap between the two ceilings. That extra caution isn't free. It's the deal you didn't get.

Swing the inputs and it flips. Stretch the hold to a year, add hard-money interest, give up a couple points in concessions, and the same band goes thin and starts blessing deals that can't pay you. It's one frozen number standing in for five moving ones.

That's the case for the full cost-stack MAO as your real ceiling: resale minus repairs, closing, holding, selling, and your profit, every dollar accounted for. Keep the 70% rule for what it is, a ten-second screen at the curb.

When the rule breaks

It fails predictably at both ends of the price range.

Expensive markets. The reserve scales with price; closing costs, holding costs, and your profit target mostly don't. Run an illustrative $600,000 ARV and the rule holds back $180,000, far more than the itemized costs and a healthy profit usually need, so its offers lose every bid. If nothing in your metro passes the screen, that isn't proof there are no deals. It's the screen.

Cheap houses. Now the reserve is too small, because some costs barely shrink with price. Illustrative numbers: ARV $90,000, repairs $25,000. The rule says offer $38,000, which is $63,000 minus $25,000. Itemize instead: $3,500 closing, $6,000 holding, $7,200 selling at 8%, and a $25,000 minimum profit, because a cheap flip still eats six months. The full stack tops out at $23,300. The rule just told you to offer $14,700 past your real ceiling, with a straight face.

Same formula, five figures wrong in both directions. It fits mid-priced deals in average conditions and quietly lies everywhere else.

Three mistakes first-time flippers make

1. Treating the ARV like a fact. Every number downstream hangs on it. Miss it by 5% on the example deal and $14,000 evaporates, nearly half the profit target, before demo day. Use sold prices of renovated comps, same beds, baths, and size, close and recent, not asking prices or an algorithm's guess at the un-renovated house. Then rerun the deal at ARV minus 5%. A deal that only works at your happiest number doesn't work.

2. Letting the calendar slide. That $7,500 of holding cost is $1,250 a month for six months. Month seven is another $1,250, plus loan interest if you're financed. Holding cost is rent you pay to own the project: the meter starts at closing and only the resale shuts it off. First-timers pad the repair budget and leave the schedule naked.

3. Creeping past the ceiling. You computed $171,600 and the seller counters at $175,000. It's only $3,400, says everyone who isn't paying it. But the MAO already contains every cost and your profit, so every dollar past it comes straight out of profit. Take the counter and you just cut your pay to $26,600 for the same six months of work. Set the walk-away number before the conversation starts.

FAQ

Is the 70% rule still realistic in 2026? As a screen, sure. As gospel, it never was. In competitive metros almost nothing passes at 70, which is why you'll hear investors quietly running 75 or 80 instead. A made-up 75 isn't more honest than a made-up 70. Itemize instead. The full stack tells you which deals work at 68% of ARV and which don't work at 80%.

What does ARV mean? After repair value: what the house should sell for once the renovation is done. Estimate it from sold prices of similar fixed-up houses nearby, same beds, baths, and square footage, sold recently. Not the as-is value, not the asking price, not a website's estimate of the wreck as it sits.

What counts as repair costs? Everything that takes the house from as-is to the condition your comps are in: materials, contractor labor, permits, dumpsters, plus contingency. Many rehabbers carry 10-15% because old houses hide surprises behind the drywall. Loan interest, taxes, insurance, and utilities don't belong here: that's holding. Commissions and concessions don't either: that's selling. Keep the buckets clean or you'll double-count one cost and miss another.
