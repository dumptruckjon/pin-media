Print on Demand Profit Margin: What a $25 Tee Actually Clears

Print on demand scatters your numbers across three screens. Revenue sits in the marketplace dashboard. Product costs sit at your print provider. Ad spend sits in the ad manager. No screen adds them up. So a shop can grow sales every month while the bank balance sits still.

This page adds them up. One $25 tee, every cost on the table, and the print on demand profit margin math that falls out of it.

The fee stack: four lines on every sale

Every print on demand sale pays the same four lines, whatever you sell and wherever you sell it. Skip a line and your margin is a story, not a number.

1. Product cost. The base garment plus the print, straight off your print provider's product page. Nobody forgets this line. It's also the only one of the four that's easy to find, which is how the other three stay hidden.

2. Marketplace fees. The example below uses Etsy's standard rates: a 6.5% transaction fee, payment processing at 3% plus $0.25, and a $0.20 listing fee. The percentages apply to the whole order, shipping charge included. Sell somewhere else and the rates change but the shape doesn't.

3. The shipping gap. What you charge for shipping and what the label costs are two different numbers. Charge $4.99 against a $3.99 label and you bank a dollar a shirt. Promise free shipping without repricing and the label comes straight out of margin. Postage rates change under you, so check the pair every month or two.

4. Ad spend per unit. A design's ad dollars for the month, divided by the units it sold. Spend $150 pushing a shirt that sold 100 and every shirt carries $1.50. This is the line sellers most often refuse to write down. Leave it out and the other three lie for it.

Worked example: the $25 tee

These are illustrative numbers, laid out so you can swap in your own. A classic tee sells for $25 plus $4.99 shipping. The blank and print cost $8.50, the label runs $3.99, and ads average $1.50 a shirt.


Sale price                                 $25.00
Shipping charged to the buyer               $4.99
Gross revenue                              $29.99

Base tee + print, your provider             $8.50
Postage the label costs                     $3.99
Transaction fee, 6.5% of $29.99             $1.95
Payment processing, 3% + $0.25              $1.15
Listing fee                                 $0.20
Ad spend, averaged per shirt                $1.50
Total costs                                $17.29

Profit per shirt                           $12.70
Margin, $12.70 / $29.99                    42.35%
Breakeven ROAS                              2.11x

The three marketplace fees take $3.30 together. Charging $4.99 against the $3.99 label hands back $1.00 of shipping profit, one of the few lines that works in your favor. So the tee clears $12.70. That's a 42.35% margin with the ad line already inside it, not the $16.50 that price-minus-blank guessing promises.

Breakeven ROAS: every product has its own

Before ads, this tee keeps $14.20 of its $29.99 order: the $12.70 it clears plus the $1.50 that went to ads. Breakeven ROAS is the order divided by that keep: $29.99 / $14.20 = 2.11x. Every ad dollar on this shirt has to bring back $2.11 of revenue. Below 2.11x, ads lose money. The listing still sells; the shop still loses.

The number is per product because the keep is per product. A design that holds onto only 30 cents of each revenue dollar needs 3.33x. Judge both designs against one shop-wide target of 2.5x and the ads report reads fine while the weak one loses on every promoted order. Compare each product to its own breakeven, never to a blended average.

A $2 raise beats 20% more sales

Raise the tee from $25 to $27. Fees take 19 cents. Only the percentage fees touch the extra $2, because product cost, postage, and ad spend don't move. You keep $1.81 of it, and the shirt now clears $14.51 instead of $12.70.

Run both growth plans over 100 shirts a month:

  • The $2 raise: 100 x $1.81 = $181 more profit. The work is editing one price field.
  • 20% more sales instead: 20 x $12.70 = $254 more profit, but only if those sales are free. Bought with ads at a 3x ROAS, each extra shirt carries $10.00 of ad cost and nets $4.20. The 20 shirts add $84.

The raise also has room to fail. At $27 the tee clears $14.51, so 88 sales out-earn the old 100: $1,277 against $1,270. The raise can lose you 12 buyers in 100 and still come out ahead. And the higher price drops breakeven ROAS from 2.11x to 2.00x, so the ads you already run get safer.

Prune the losers

A shop-level margin is an average, and averages hide losers. A 20% shop margin can be six healthy designs and two that lose money on every order. Sort your products by margin and read the bottom of the table.

The flag is a row whose breakeven ROAS sits above what its ads really return. This tee goes red past $14.20 of ad spend per shirt. At an actual 1.5x ROAS, a $29.99 order took $19.99 of ads and lost $5.79.

A losing row gets one of three moves: raise its price, cut its ad spend, or retire the design. Retiring a loser doesn't shrink the business. It stops a leak.

Three margin mistakes POD sellers make

1. Copying competitor prices. Their $25 sits on their cost stack, not yours. Print the same design on a $12 premium blank instead of an $8.50 classic and the same price clears $9.20, not $12.70. Price from your own stack. Their listing can do what it likes.

2. Free shipping without repricing. Flip the $25 tee to free shipping and $4.52 a shirt walks away. That's the $4.99 you stopped collecting, less the 47 cents of fees it used to carry. Free shipping is a pricing decision, not a checkbox. Fold the label into the item price first.

3. Measuring margin before ads. Without its ad line this tee reads $14.20 and looks untouchable. It clears $12.70, and only while ads hold near $1.50 a shirt. A rising sales graph over a flat payout is usually this mistake.

FAQ

What is a good profit margin for print on demand? Measure it after all four lines, ads included: profit divided by gross revenue. I aim for 30% or better; the example tee's 42.35% is what headroom looks like. Under 20%, one base-cost bump or a postage change can flip a row negative.

What is breakeven ROAS? The ad return where a promoted sale stops losing money: gross revenue divided by what the sale keeps before ad spend. The example tee keeps $14.20 of $29.99, so its breakeven is 2.11x. Every product has its own. Judge a design's ads against its own number, not the shop's blended average.

How much does a print on demand shirt cost to make? Whatever your provider's page quotes for the base garment plus print. Here that's $8.50 for a classic tee. Premium blanks, larger sizes, and a second print location all raise it. Making cost is barely half the story anyway: on this tee, postage, fees, and ads add another $8.79 before a cent is profit.
