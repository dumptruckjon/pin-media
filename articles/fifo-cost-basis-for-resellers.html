FIFO for Resellers: Why Your Profit Numbers Are Probably Wrong

You paid $40 for the bin. Thirty shirts, give or take. One sells on Poshmark for $18, you call the cost "like a buck," and the flip made $17. Except it didn't. The fee came out, the guess was a guess, and by April nobody remembers what that bin owed you.

Resellers rarely lose money sourcing. They lose track of it. FIFO is the boring fix, and one example is enough to learn it.

Why buying in lots breaks your profit math

You buy by the lot and sell by the item. The receipt shows one number, and the profit math needs a different one. Goodwill doesn't itemize.

So everyone improvises:

  • Divide the lot price evenly, then forget the number by next week.
  • Count the whole bin against the first sale, then call everything after it pure profit.
  • Guess fresh each time, always in the direction that feels good.

A wrong cost basis doesn't stay small. It flows into every number downstream: profit per flip, margin, which platform deserves your time, and what you owe in April.

What FIFO actually means for a reseller

First in, first out: the first unit you bought is the first unit you count as sold. That's the whole rule.

Make it concrete. In March you pull 10 denim jackets from Goodwill at $8 each. In May an estate sale coughs up 6 more at $11. Same rack, same listing photos, different cost.

You sell 4 jackets. FIFO counts them from the March lot: 4 x $8 = $32. You sell 3 more. The March lot still has 6 left, so the cost is 3 x $8 = $24. The May jackets haven't entered the math at all. They wait their turn. Only when all 10 of the $8 jackets are used up does a jacket start costing you $11.

No memory required, no mood involved. Just a written lot log and a rule that answers the same way every time.

Fees and shipping hit each platform differently

Cost basis is half the problem. The other half is what the platform keeps, and each one keeps it differently.

  • Poshmark: the buyer pays for the shipping label by default, so a standard sale ships free for you.
  • eBay: the fee is a percentage of the whole checkout, plus a small per-order charge. Offer free shipping and the label comes out of your pocket too.
  • Mercari: the fee schedule has been rewritten more than once in the past few years. Whatever number you memorized is probably stale.

Watch what that does in the example below. The eBay jackets grossed $112 and netted $55.65 after cost, fees, and a $9.50 label. The Poshmark jackets grossed $96 and netted $62.40. Smaller sale, bigger profit.

So never rank platforms by sale price. And never estimate fees from a remembered percentage: record what each platform actually took, order by order.

The numbers that matter at tax time

Schedule C wants totals you can defend:

  • Gross receipts: every dollar buyers paid you.
  • Cost of goods sold: the FIFO cost of the items that sold this year, not what you spent sourcing this year.
  • Fees and shipping: selling costs you deduct, tracked separately from your item costs.
  • Ending inventory: what's still on the shelf, valued at what it cost you.

The second line trips people up the most. A bin bought in December isn't a December expense. Its cost counts in the year each item sells.

Here's the pressure behind all this: platforms report your gross sales to the IRS on a 1099-K. The reporting threshold keeps moving, but the form never says profit. Getting from the gross number to the profit number is your job, and FIFO plus clean fee records is the path.

I build trackers, not tax returns. Have your accountant confirm the method fits your situation.

A worked example, line by line

Here are the books for a small stretch of flipping: four lots bought, three sales across three platforms. Every number is laid out so you can swap in your own.


Purchases (four lots, $226.00 total)
  10 denim jackets, Goodwill, $8.00 each        $80.00
  6 denim jackets, estate sale, $11.00 each     $66.00
  8 pairs of Nike runners, bins, $5.50 each     $44.00
  12 board games, thrift store, $3.00 each      $36.00

Sales
  eBay: 4 jackets at $28.00                    $112.00
  Poshmark: 3 jackets at $32.00                 $96.00
  Mercari: 2 pairs of shoes at $45.00           $90.00
  Gross revenue                                $298.00

FIFO cost of goods sold
  7 jackets, all from the $8.00 Goodwill lot    $56.00
  2 pairs of shoes at $5.50                     $11.00
  COGS                                          $67.00

Selling costs
  Platform fees: $14.85 + $9.60 + $4.50         $28.95
  Shipping you paid: $9.50 + $0 + $8.00         $17.50

Net profit: $298.00 - $67.00 - $28.95 - $17.50 = $184.55
Margin: $184.55 / $298.00 = 61.93%
ROI: $184.55 / $67.00 = 275.45%

Three things worth noticing.

The $11 estate-sale jackets never show up in COGS. Three $8 jackets are still on the shelf, so FIFO hasn't reached the second lot yet.

Mercari grossed the least and kept the most: $66.50 net on $90. eBay grossed the most and kept the least: $55.65 net on $112. Sale prices alone would have ranked them backwards.

And of the $226 spent sourcing, only $67 has become cost of goods sold. The other $159 is ending inventory, sitting on the shelf at cost, waiting for its year.

Three mistakes that skew reseller profit

1. Expensing the lot on the day you buy it. The $226 in purchases isn't $226 in expenses. Until an item sells, its cost is inventory. Deduct it early and this year's profit reads low, next year's reads high, and neither is real.

2. Treating gross as income. These books gross $298.00 and keep $184.55. The $113.45 gap is cost, fees, and labels, about 38 cents of every dollar buyers paid. Price your next sourcing trip off the gross number and that gap arrives as a surprise.

3. A fresh guess on every sale. "Call it five bucks" assigns whatever cost feels right that day. Guesses drift. A year of drift can't be untangled in April, because nobody wrote anything down. A lot log plus FIFO answers once, in writing.

FAQ

Do resellers have to use FIFO? No. If every item is unique and you know exactly what it cost, specific identification works, and plenty of vintage sellers do just that. FIFO earns its keep when you buy similar items in lots and per-item cost would be a guess. Pick one method, apply it consistently, and confirm the choice with your accountant.

What happens to items that haven't sold yet? Nothing, and that's the point. They sit in ending inventory at cost, the $159 in the example above. Each item's cost enters COGS in the year it sells. A death pile isn't deductible just because it's depressing.

Which matters more, margin or ROI? Margin is profit over revenue: 61.93% above. ROI is profit over what the sold items cost you: 275.45%. ROI is the one I'd watch, because sourcing cash is the thing a reseller runs out of first. The margin says your pricing works. The ROI says every dollar of sold inventory came back with $2.75 riding on top.
