Seller Net Sheet, Explained: What You Actually Walk Away With

Every seller runs the same math. Sale price minus the mortgage, and that's my check. On the $450,000 sale below, that math promises $155,000. The wire that actually lands is $123,495. The gap is $31,505.

None of it is hidden. It's eight ordinary cost lines, every one knowable before the sign goes in the yard. A seller net sheet is the page that lists them: everything between the sale price and the money that reaches your account.

Agents run one at the listing appointment. You can run your own first. Here's every line, with real numbers throughout.

Every line between the sale price and the wire

The costs come in five groups.

1. The mortgage payoff, which is not your app balance. A mortgage accrues interest daily, and the payoff quote runs through the date the lender receives funds, plus a small recording or wire fee. On a $295,000 loan at 6%, per-diem interest runs about $48 a day, so fifteen days between statement and closing adds roughly $727. Order the payoff quote in writing and put that number on the sheet, not your app balance.

2. Commission, both sides of it. Whatever you agreed to pay your listing agent, plus anything you offer toward the buyer's agent. Both are percentages of the price, the biggest true cost on nearly every sheet. Below they're 3% and 2.5%: $24,750 combined. Selling without an agent (FSBO) deletes the listing line only; most FSBO sellers still offer the buyer's side something, and every other line stays.

3. Title, escrow, and transfer tax. Three things live here: the buyer's title policy (seller-paid in many states), the escrow or attorney fee split, and the state or county transfer tax. The example carries $1,450 of title and escrow plus a 0.15% transfer tax, $675. Some places tax at ten times that rate. Look up yours.

4. Prorated property tax. You owe your slice of the tax period through closing day. Here that's 73 days of a $5,400 tax year, which is $1,080. Depending on your state's billing cycle, this line can shrink or flip into a credit back to you.

5. Concessions, repairs, and the HOA. This group is zero on listing day and real money by closing. It covers:

  • the repair credit you negotiate after the inspection
  • a home warranty, if you offer the buyer one
  • any help you agree to give toward the buyer's closing costs
  • HOA dues proration and the association's transfer fee, if you have one

In the example it lands at $3,550, and no sheet run before the offer exists can show it.

Why the agent's verbal estimate misses

The number quoted at your kitchen table is honest and stale on arrival. A verbal estimate rounds the commission, reads your loan balance instead of a payoff quote, and skips the prorations. And it's made weeks before the inspection.

The closing statement is computed to the day, after every negotiation is finished. Of course they disagree.

The fix is paper and timing, not a better agent. Ask for the net sheet in writing at the listing appointment, then a fresh one with every offer and after the inspection response. Each rerun takes about a minute.

The worked example, line by line

These are the numbers my calculator opens with, laid out so you can swap in your own. Sale price $450,000, one mortgage payoff of $295,000. Commission 3% on the listing side, 2.5% offered to the buyer's agent. Title and escrow $1,450, transfer tax 0.15% of the price. Property tax of $5,400 a year, prorated for 73 days. A $3,000 repair credit and a $550 home warranty finish it. Here's the whole sheet.


Sale price                                 $450,000.00

Mortgage payoff (1st lien)                 $295,000.00
Listing agent commission, 3%                $13,500.00
Buyer agent concession, 2.5%                $11,250.00
Title and escrow fees                        $1,450.00
Transfer tax, 0.15% of price                   $675.00
Prorated property tax, 73 days of $5,400     $1,080.00
Repair credits to the buyer                  $3,000.00
Home warranty                                  $550.00
Total costs                                $326,505.00

Net to seller                              $123,495.00

The sheet reads that net as 27.44% of the sale price. True selling costs are $31,505, almost exactly 7% of the price. The other $295,000 was a loan you owed whether or not you sold. But "price minus mortgage" promised $155,000, and a seller who mentally spent that number arrives at the wire $31,505 short.

Run the scenarios before you set a listing price

Percentage lines scale with the price. Fixed lines don't. So "what would we net at a lower price" is a computable question. Compute it before you pick the listing price, not mid-negotiation.

The example at three asking prices: $435,000 nets $109,342.50. $450,000 nets $123,495.00. $465,000 nets $137,647.50.

Look at the spacing. Each $15,000 step moves the net by exactly $14,152.50, because the commission, concession, and transfer tax skim 5.65% of every marginal dollar. You keep the other 94.35 cents. A $15,000 price cut costs $14,152.50 of net, a number worth knowing before the price-drop conversation.

Before you list, find the price where the net stops covering what the money is for. Next down payment, second lien, the number you promised yourself. That's your floor. Knowing it is the one habit on this page I'd push on every seller.

Three surprises sellers hit at closing

1. The payoff beats the balance. Per-diem interest runs to the funding date, not your last statement, and a lender fee rides along. Budget a few hundred dollars above the app number. Your escrow account isn't lost, though. The lender refunds it separately a few weeks after closing, and it never appears on the net sheet.

2. You're paying people on the buyer's side. The buyer agent concession, the repair credit, and the warranty above add up to $14,800. Not one of those dollars existed on listing day. That's what negotiation does, and it's the reason the sheet gets re-run with every offer.

3. The small lines that never come up early. Transfer tax plus the tax proration is $1,755 in the example, and a live closing usually adds a recording fee, a courier fee, maybe an HOA transfer charge. Each sounds trivial alone. Itemized on one page, they finally register.

FAQ

What is a seller net sheet? An itemized estimate of what a home seller walks away with. It starts at the price and subtracts the payoff, commissions and concessions, title and escrow fees, transfer tax, prorations, and credits to the buyer. Agents prepare one at listing appointments and with each offer. There's nothing in it you can't run yourself first.

How much do I get if I sell my house? Sale price, minus four things: the written payoff quote, both commissions, the closing fees and taxes, and whatever you agreed to credit or fix. In the worked example a $450,000 sale nets $123,495, after a $295,000 payoff and $31,505 of selling costs. Your mix will differ. Ten minutes with your own numbers beats any rule of thumb.

Who pays closing costs? Both sides carry their own set. Sellers typically pay the commissions, the transfer tax in many states, part of the escrow fee, and whatever credits they negotiated. Buyers typically pay their loan fees, appraisal, and inspection. Nearly every line is negotiable: when a buyer asks you to cover part of their costs, it lands on your sheet as a concession. The sheet tells you whether the deal still clears your floor.
