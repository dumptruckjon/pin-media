Is Your Farmers Market Booth Actually Profitable?

Most vendors can quote Saturday's sales to the dollar. Ask what the day paid per hour and the table goes quiet.

Farmers market profit isn't the cash box at 2pm. It's what's left after four costs, and most vendors only ever total one of them. Run one Saturday honestly. All you need is this page and a phone calculator.

What one market day really costs

Four lines. Skip any of them and the day looks better than it was.

1. Booth fee. The easy one, because someone makes you pay it. Twenty dollars at a small market, seventy-five or more at a festival. It comes off the top whether you sell out or sit in the rain.

2. Product cost. What the goods on the table cost to make: fruit, sugar, jars, lids, labels, flour. Count it for every unit that sells. And watch the perishables. Jam that doesn't sell comes home as inventory. Bread that doesn't sell comes home as waste. A loaf is spent when you bake it, not when someone buys it.

3. Hours, at a wage you'd say out loud. The selling window is the smallest piece. A real market day includes:

  • prep and baking the day before
  • loading, driving there, setup
  • the selling hours themselves
  • breakdown, driving home, unloading

A market that runs 9am to 1pm is rarely under six hours door to door, and a baking day can double that. If you wouldn't work a stranger's booth for $4 an hour, stop letting yours pay it.

4. Mileage. The IRS rate is 70 cents a mile as I write this, and it's a fair proxy for gas plus wear. An 18-mile round trip costs $12.60. Do that forty Saturdays and the van quietly billed you $504.

The per-hour truth

A big day can still pay badly. Here's a $400 Saturday, the kind most vendors call a win:


Revenue                                    $400.00

Booth fee                                   $60.00
Ingredients and packaging                  $160.00
Mileage, 50 miles at $0.70                  $35.00
Bags, ice, card fees                        $10.00
Total costs                                $265.00

Cash profit                                $135.00

Hours: 5 baking + 0.5 loading + 1.5 driving
+ 0.75 setup + 5 selling + 0.75 breakdown
+ 0.5 unloading                             14.0 h

Wage: $135.00 / 14 hours                  $9.64/hr

Four hundred dollars in sales, and the day paid $9.64 an hour. Revenue is not a wage. The cash box shows the first number and hides the second. That's how a booth feels great all summer while paying less than the grocery store.

Worked example: one Saturday at Maple Street

You bring 24 jars of strawberry jam at $8, 10 sourdough loaves at $12, and 8 jars of wildflower honey at $15. By close you've sold 5 jam, 3 bread, and 2 honey.


Revenue
  Strawberry jam      5 x $8               $40.00
  Sourdough loaf      3 x $12              $36.00
  Wildflower honey    2 x $15              $30.00
  Total                                   $106.00

Expenses
  Booth fee                                $35.00
  Mileage             18 mi x $0.70        $12.60
  Supplies                                  $5.00
  Total                                    $52.60

Profit                                     $53.40

Hours: 0.5 travel + 0.75 setup
+ 4 selling + 0.5 breakdown                5.75 h

Wage: $53.40 / 5.75 hours                $9.29/hr

That's the kind number. Two honest add-ons take it further:

Ingredients. Say the jam costs $2.50 a jar to make, the bread $2 a loaf, the honey $1.50 a jar in packaging. The ten sold units consumed $21.50, so profit drops to $31.90 and the wage to $5.55 an hour.

Prep time. Add two hours of Thursday jam-making and it falls to $4.12 an hour. If the seven unsold loaves go stale, there's another $14 gone.

None of this means quit. It means change what you bring, where you sell, and what you charge. All three are fixable. Every fix starts with one day's numbers written down.

Track sell-through, then pack a lighter van

Sell-through is units sold divided by units brought. Same Saturday:

  • Strawberry jam: 5 of 24 sold, 21%
  • Wildflower honey: 2 of 8 sold, 25%
  • Sourdough loaf: 3 of 10 sold, 30%

Jam won the revenue column at $40. Bread won sell-through at 30%. And nothing cracked a third: the van carried 42 units to sell 10. Ten sold, thirty-two rode home.

Overpacking isn't free, even when the product keeps. It costs prep hours and cooler space, and for bread it's flour you'll throw away. Track three or four weeks and the pattern gets loud. Bring 10 jars of jam instead of 24, bake 6 loaves instead of 10, and bank the prep hours you just got back. Sell-through is the most useful number in market selling, and almost nobody computes it.

Two markets? Compare wages, not cash boxes

Say you alternate two Saturday markets for a month and track both:

  • Riverside, 15 minutes away: average profit $153, 8.5 hours door to door. That's $18.00 an hour.
  • Downtown, 45 minutes away: average profit $198, 12 hours door to door. That's $16.50 an hour.

Downtown wins the cash box by $45. Riverside wins the wage. If Saturdays are scarce, Riverside is the better job. The 3.5 hours it hands back beat the extra $45 any week there's baking waiting.

Two cautions here. Don't judge a market on one week, because a single rained-out Saturday can slander a good spot. And write the weather next to each day's profit, so you know whether Downtown is genuinely soft or just caught three wet weekends in a row.

Three mistakes market sellers make

1. Calling revenue profit. The cash box holds $400, so dinner gets ordered, and the booth fee, ingredients, and gas quietly ate $265 of it. Total one full day honestly and this habit breaks for good.

2. Working for free. Friday baking, the dawn load-in, the teardown in August heat. Those hours are the biggest cost of a market day and the only one that never hands you a receipt. Bill yourself anyway. A booth that only profits when your time is worth zero isn't profitable.

3. Packing on hope instead of data. The van has room, so 24 jars ride along, and then prep eats the whole week. Let last Saturday's sell-through decide what rides to this one. Hope is not an inventory plan.

FAQ

How much do farmers market vendors make? Ask ten vendors and you'll get ten answers, most of them gross sales, not profit. Revenue runs from under $100 at a slow small-town market to four figures at a busy city one. Wage is the better question. Both worked examples on this page landed under $10 an hour, and neither had an unusual cost in it. Total your own day; it beats anyone's average.

What sells best at farmers markets? The general pattern is consumables people finish and buy again: bread, produce, preserves. But the general pattern won't pack your van. The jam above out-earned everything at $40 and still sat at 21% sell-through. Track sold versus brought per product for a month. Your own table is the only answer that pays.

Are farmers markets worth it? Sometimes. Selling at farmers markets also earns things the cash box can't show: regulars, wholesale leads, a live test bench for new products. Those are worth real money, but give each one a number and a deadline. "Downtown pays $16.50 an hour and produced two wholesale accounts" is a strategy. "It's good exposure" is how a hobby stays a hobby.
