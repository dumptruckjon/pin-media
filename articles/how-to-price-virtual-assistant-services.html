How to Price Virtual Assistant Services: Hourly, Packages, and the Rate That Pays You

Most virtual assistant rates come from a group chat. Someone says they charge $30, so you charge $28 to be safe. Six months later you're booked, tired, and somehow still short at tax time.

The problem isn't effort. A flat hourly guess ignores taxes, tools, and every hour nobody pays for. Here's how to price virtual assistant services backward from the only number that matters: what you actually keep.

Work backward from take-home, not forward from a guess

An employee's $30 an hour and your $30 an hour are different currencies. An employer covers payroll tax, software, and the slow weeks. You cover all of it from the same rate.

So start at the end and walk back. Four lines:

1. Adjusted billable hours per week = committed hours x (1 - admin leakage). Leakage is the slice of your week spent on proposals, invoicing, and the inbox. No client pays for it, and ignoring it doesn't shrink it. 2. Annual billable hours = adjusted hours x (52 - vacation weeks). You'll take time off, or the flu will take it for you. Price 52 working weeks and you've priced a year you won't live. 3. Gross revenue needed = (take-home goal + annual overhead) / (1 - tax set-aside). Overhead is the boring monthly stuff: scheduling software, a password manager, business insurance. The division grosses up for taxes, which come out before you see a dime. 4. Minimum hourly rate = gross revenue needed / annual billable hours.

That last number is a floor, not a price. Charge under it and you're paying clients for the privilege of working.

Your billable hours are fewer than you think

Here's the ceiling most VAs never compute. Plan 30 client hours a week with 20% admin leakage and your real capacity is 24 billable hours, about 104 a month. Not 130.

That 104 does two jobs. It sets your rate, and it caps your income. Book 45 client hours a month and you sit at 43.27% utilization: room to grow, no reason to panic-discount. Add a prospective 20-hour client at $80 an hour and utilization moves to 62.5%, worth $1,600 more a month. Past 90% there are no hours left to sell, and the only raise left is the rate itself.

Of the five inputs, leakage is the one nobody has measured. Measure it. Track one honest week, count only the minutes a client pays for, and believe the result over the plan.

Worked example: the $60,000 VA

These are the defaults my calculator ships with, laid out so you can swap in your own numbers. The goal: $60,000 a year in your pocket, on 30 committed client hours a week with 4 weeks off. Admin leakage runs 20%, the tax set-aside is 28%, and overhead is $100 a month for software and tools plus $50 for business insurance.


Take-home goal                             $60,000
Overhead: $150/mo x 12                      $1,800
Gross revenue needed: $61,800 / 0.72       $85,833

Committed hours                          30 hrs/wk
After 20% admin leakage: 30 x 0.80    24.00 hrs/wk
Working weeks: 52 - 4 vacation                  48
Annual billable hours: 24 x 48               1,152

Minimum rate: $85,833 / 1,152            $74.51/hr

Quote $75 and close the loop: $75 x 1,152 hours brings in $86,400. Set aside 28% and $62,208 remains. Pay the $1,800 of overhead and your take-home is $60,408. Goal hit, with a small cushion for the week a client ghosts.

Now compare the guess. $60,000 spread over a 40-hour, 52-week year is $28.85 an hour, the number group-chat pricing anchors on. It isn't even half the floor you just computed. The gap isn't greed. It's taxes, tools, and unpaid admin time, none of which the guess ever met.

Hourly or packages: when each one wins

Hourly wins when scope is fog: a brand-new client, a cleanup project, "just help me get organized." You can't fix a price on work you can't see yet. Bill the hours and let the first month teach you the shape of the work.

Packages win once the work repeats, like 10 hours of inbox and calendar every month. The client gets a predictable invoice. You get committed hours and fewer scope arguments.

One rule keeps packages honest: discount from a base rate above your floor, never from the floor itself. Watch what happens otherwise. Take the $74.51 floor, sell 20 hours at a 10% discount, and the tier prices at $1,340 a month, an effective $67.00 an hour. That's $7.51 under minimum, about $1,800 a year handed back. Small discount, real money. Set the tier from your market rate, then check the effective rate still clears the floor.

Raising rates without losing the roster

Utilization decides the move. Sitting at 43%? Sell the empty hours first and quote every new inquiry at the new rate. New clients never met the old one, so a raise costs nothing to test.

Near capacity, raise the roster. Plain email, 30 to 60 days of notice, no apology tour: "Starting September 1 my rate moves from $75 to $85 an hour. Your scope and turnaround don't change. I wanted to give you two months of notice rather than a surprise."

One raise a year is plenty. Some clients will say no, and the math makes that survivable: lose 15 hours at $75 and it takes about 14 at $80 to earn it back. And skip permanent grandfathering. A legacy rate is a discount with no end date, and every new client quietly pays for it.

Three ways virtual assistants underprice

1. Borrowing someone else's rate. The $28 from the group chat carries that VA's tax bracket, their overhead, their hours. None of those are yours. Your floor comes out of your own numbers or it isn't a floor.

2. Selling hours that don't exist. A plan with 40 billable hours assumes nobody invoices, markets, or answers email. At 20% leakage, 30 committed hours are 24 in practice. Price the 24, and protect them.

3. Discounting below the floor. Every package tier has an effective rate: price divided by hours. Compute it before the client ever sees the number. A tier under your minimum is a subscription to losing money, renewed monthly.

FAQ

How much should a virtual assistant charge? Run your floor first: (take-home goal + overhead) / (1 - tax set-aside) / real billable hours. The example above lands at $74.51 an hour against a $60,000 goal. Specialized work such as bookkeeping, ads management, or podcast production sells above general admin, so the market band may sit higher than your floor. Charge toward the market. The floor only marks where yes stops paying you.

How many billable hours a week can a VA actually work? Fewer than the calendar says. Committed hours minus admin leakage: 30 committed at 20% leakage is 24 billable, about 104 a month. Before promising more, track one week and count only paid minutes. Whatever that week says, trust it over the plan.

Should a VA charge hourly or sell packages? Hourly for new clients and fuzzy scope. Packages once the work is repeatable and you want predictable income. Price the package from a base above your minimum and check the effective rate, because hours plus discount can quietly sink a tier below the floor.
