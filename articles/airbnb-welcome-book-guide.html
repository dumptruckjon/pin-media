What to Put in an Airbnb Welcome Book (and What to Leave Out)

Every host answers the same five questions. What's the WiFi password. How does the door code work. When is checkout. Where does the trash go. Where should we eat.

You typed the answers last stay and the stay before that. An Airbnb welcome book is where those answers live instead of your text thread. Done right it's short, skimmable, and read standing in the entryway with bags still in hand. Here's what goes in, what stays out, and the one rule that keeps it working.

The eight sections that stop repeat questions

Cover these eight and the texts mostly stop.

1. WiFi. Network name and password, spelled exactly the way the router does, capitals included. Put it first: it's the first thing every guest hunts for. Add one fix-it line: unplug the router, count to ten, plug it back in.

2. Check-in, checkout, and access. Both times, the door code or lockbox spot, and where to park. Then the backup plan: what to do if the code won't take, and your number. Write this section like you're calming someone in a dark driveway, because someday you are.

3. House rules. Quiet hours, smoking, pets, max guests, parties. One plain line each, with the reason when there is one: "Quiet hours after 10, the neighbors have young kids." Five or six lines total. It's a home, not a lease.

4. How things work. Only the machines guests touch: thermostat, TV and streaming, washer, dishwasher, the shower with the trick handle. One or two lines each. If something needs a full paragraph, it needs a printed label taped next to the thing itself.

5. Trash day. Which bin, where the bins live, which night they roll to the curb, and what the town refuses to collect. This is the question that arrives at 9 PM on a Sunday. Answer it in advance.

6. Emergency info. The property's street address first, because a guest calling for help often can't remember it. Then the nearest urgent care, the fire extinguisher spot, the water shutoff, and a backup contact besides you. Nobody reads this section until they need it. That's fine. It has to be there anyway.

7. Local recommendations. Five favorites, not fifty. Coffee, breakfast, dinner, one thing to do, one place only locals know. One honest line about why each earns its spot beats a pasted tourist brochure.

8. The checkout list. Three to five small tasks: trash out, dishes started, doors locked, keys back where they came from. Keep it short on purpose. A ten-chore checkout list reads like a second cleaning fee, and guests notice.

What to leave out

The things that get a welcome book ignored:

  • Walls of text. Nobody reads paragraph six. Guests skim headings looking for one answer, and a section that can't be skimmed may as well not exist.
  • The liability lecture. Rules written like a rental contract make guests bristle and skip ahead. Your platform terms and rental agreement carry the legal weight. The book is hospitality.
  • The 40-page PDF. A photo-heavy manual feels thorough to write and gets closed by page two. Length isn't care. Findability is care.
  • Anything that goes stale weekly. Event calendars, seasonal hours, pop-ups. If you won't maintain it, don't print it.

The two-minute rule

A welcome book only works if guests finish it. My target is about a two minute read, front to back. That's roughly what a tired guest with a phone in one hand will give you, and it's plenty for eight short sections.

The test is simple. Read your book out loud at normal speed. Where you drone, cut. Move the overflow onto a label on the appliance or a line in your check-in message. Short is a feature.

Digital or printed? Both.

Send the digital copy before arrival, in the booking message or as a listing link. The WiFi password is in their pocket before they reach the door.

Keep a printed copy in the rental too, on the counter or in a small binder. It covers the guest whose phone is dead and the one who never opens links. Same content, two containers. When the door code or trash night changes, update both the same day.

Three mistakes hosts make

1. Writing it once and never touching it again. The router gets replaced, the pizza place closes, the door code rotates, and the book still says otherwise. One stale answer and guests stop trusting every page. Re-read it after any change to the house, and once a season regardless.

2. Burying the answer. A WiFi password hidden mid-paragraph on page four might as well not exist. One topic per heading, the password on its own line, access details where a thumb finds them fast. If guests still text you a question the book answers, that's data. Move the answer up.

3. Writing for lawyers instead of guests. "Use of the hot tub constitutes acceptance of" reads like a deposition. Guests skip it, and the warmth you built everywhere else drains out. Write rules the way you'd say them across the kitchen counter. A book that sounds like a person gets treated like a person's home.

FAQ

What should be in an Airbnb welcome book? Eight sections: WiFi, check-in and checkout with access details, house rules, how the appliances work, trash day, emergency info, local recommendations, and a short checkout list. If it answers the questions guests text you, it's complete.

How long should an Airbnb welcome book be? About a two minute read. A handful of skimmable sections beats a 40-page manual, because the manual gets closed early and the questions come to your phone anyway.

Should a welcome book be printed or digital? Both. A digital copy sent before arrival puts the answers in the guest's pocket, and a printed copy in the rental covers dead phones and guests who won't open links. Update both whenever anything changes.
