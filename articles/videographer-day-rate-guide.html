Videographer Day Rate: Work Backward From the Life You Want

Most videographer day rates are borrowed. You looked up three competitors, picked the middle number, and hoped. Copying feels safe. It isn't. That number encodes someone else's rent, someone else's paid-off gear, someone else's year. The market can't know what your life has to cost.

A videographer day rate should be an output, not an input. Start from the income you want to keep, then work backward to the rate that delivers it. The math takes five inputs. Here it is, with every line shown.

Start from net, not the market

A day rate isn't really a price. It's a salary delivery system. Whatever you charge has to turn into rent, groceries, and retirement after every business cost gets paid. So the first number isn't a rate at all. It's your target net income: what you personally keep this year.

The market still matters, but it comes last, as a positioning check. Compute the rate your life requires, then check whether your niche can carry it. If your number sits above the going rate, that's information about your business model, not an order to charge less. Copying the average buys you someone else's average life.

The backward math

Write down four numbers before any rate exists.

  • Target net income. What actually lands in your pocket.
  • Annual overhead. Insurance, software subscriptions, cloud storage, the drive shelf.
  • Gear recovery. What this year must bank toward replacing equipment (next section).
  • Tax set-aside. The percent of every invoice you park for taxes. 30% is a common self-employment starting point; your accountant beats my default.

Then the math runs in one direction:

Gross needed = (net + overhead + gear recovery) / (1 - tax set-aside)

Minimum day rate = gross needed / billable shoot days

Divide, don't multiply. Dividing by 0.70 grosses up correctly; adding 30% on top does not, and the shortfall comes out of your pocket. One honest note: this model sets aside tax on every gross dollar, even the ones that pay deductible overhead. It's deliberately conservative. A surprise refund beats a surprise bill.

Why 80 billable days, not 250

There are about 250 working days in a year. You won't shoot 250 of them. Every shoot day drags hidden days behind it:

  • the edit, often 1-2 days per shoot day depending on deliverables
  • proposals, calls, and emails, including for jobs that never book
  • ingest, backups, gear maintenance, invoicing
  • the marketing that keeps next month alive

Two shoot days a week for most of the year is already a busy calendar, and that's roughly 90-100 days. Eighty is an honest planning number for a working freelancer. A third of the year behind the camera, two thirds running the business that puts you there. Divide by 250 instead and you build a rate that only works if you never edit, never sell, and never rest. That rate fails in February.

Your camera is a per-shoot cost

Gear feels like a purchase. It behaves like rent. A $3,600 camera body you'll replace in three years isn't a one-time hit. It's a cost attached to every shoot between now and the replacement. At 80 shoot days a year, three years is 240 shoots: $15 a shoot, every shoot. The same math applies to lenses, lights, audio, and the drone you pretend is for fun.

Price each item by dividing what it cost by the shoots it has left, then add up the lines. That's your per-shoot gear cost. Multiplied across the year's shoot days, that becomes gear recovery. It's the line that fills the camera fund on purpose, instead of raiding savings every third autumn. In the worked example below it comes to $4,877.93 for the year, about $61 a shoot.

The 20% buffer

The minimum rate has no slack in it. It assumes every invoice pays, no shoot cancels, nothing breaks early, and you're never sick in Q4. That list never survives contact. So the recommended rate adds a 20% sustainability buffer, and the good years bank the difference. Charge the minimum and one bad month is a crisis. Charge the buffered rate and it's an annoyance.

Half days cost more than half

Price a half day at 60% of the full rate, not 50%. The client buys four hours of shooting. Your fixed work doesn't shrink: same kit packing, same drive, same setup and teardown, same backups that night. Setup and travel don't halve. A half-day booking also tends to block the whole day from other work. The 60% floor charges for the fixed parts; only the shooting got shorter.

Three ways videographers underprice

1. Dividing by too many days. A $75,000 gross target over 250 days quotes $300 and feels plausible. Over a realistic 80 days it's $937.50. The denominator decides everything.

2. Treating paid-off gear as free. The camera survived last year, so this year it costs nothing, right? Then it dies mid-season and the replacement lands on a credit card. The $15-a-shoot version of that camera was always cheaper than the emergency version. No gear line in your rate means your gear fund is your savings account.

3. Copying a rate without copying the business. The shooter charging $650 might be part-time, with paid-off gear and insurance through a spouse. Their rate can't know your net target, your overhead, or your day count. Build your own floor first, then use the market for positioning, not pricing.

FAQ

How much should a videographer charge per day?

Whatever your backward math says, not a universal number. Add target net, overhead, and gear recovery, divide by 1 minus your tax set-aside, then divide by billable days. The worked example lands at $1,244.25 minimum and $1,493.10 recommended for a $60,000 net target. A different life produces a different rate. That's the point.

What is a half day rate?

Roughly four hours on site, priced at about 60% of your full day rate. Packing, travel, setup, and backups take the same time whether you shoot four hours or nine. And the booking usually eats the rest of the day anyway. From the example above: $746.55 minimum, $895.86 with the buffer.

How many billable days a year is realistic for a freelance videographer?

Plan around 80-100. Every shoot day needs edit days, proposals, and admin beside it, so two shoot days a week is already a full calendar. Early on you'll book fewer while the pipeline builds. Consistently shooting past 100? Raise the rate. Demand is telling you something.
