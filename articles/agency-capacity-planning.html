Should You Take That New Client? (Agency Capacity Math in One Screen)

A $5,000 retainer lands in your inbox. The work fits, the logo would look good on your site, and the quarter's been slow. So you take it. Two months later your best designer is booked past 103% and the profit you pictured never reaches the account.

Small agencies rarely die from bad work. They die from good clients accepted without math. This page is that math: agency capacity planning in four numbers, then a real example worked line by line.

Revenue per client lies

Rank your clients by retainer and you'll protect the wrong ones. Revenue is the loudest number and the least honest.

What a client really costs is two numbers multiplied, per person:

  • Allocated hours: the hours each team member actually spends on that client in a month.
  • Loaded rate: what an hour of that person costs you. Wage plus payroll taxes, software seats, and the management time nobody bills. Not the billable rate, and never the bare wage.

Cost to serve = allocated hours x loaded rate, summed across everyone who touches the account.

Here's a three-person studio you'll see again below. Alex costs $45 an hour loaded, Jordan $55, Casey $35. A client taking 40 Alex hours and 20 Jordan hours costs $2,900 a month to serve. Whether it pays $5,000 or $3,000 decides everything. The invoice total says nothing.

Utilization is the real constraint

Money runs out slowly. Hours run out on a Tuesday.

Utilization is allocated hours divided by capacity hours, per person. In the studio above, Alex is allocated 125 of 160 hours, which is 78.13%. Jordan sits at 60 of 120, an even 50.00%. Casey runs 115 of 160, or 71.88%.

Average the team and you get 300 of 440 hours, 68.18%. Sounds roomy. But prospects don't buy your average, they buy Alex. Design-heavy deals stack onto the same person, and the average hides it until the resignation email.

Someone always hits 100% before the money runs out. Agency capacity planning is knowing who, and how many hours from now.

Model the client before you say yes

The discipline is simple: no retainer gets a verbal yes until you've typed it in as numbers. The retainer amount, plus the hours you'd pull from each person. Thirty seconds of typing.

That's the whole what-if habit. Two questions, every time:

1. What does this client cost to serve, and what margin is left? 2. Whose utilization does it break?

Question two is the one agencies skip. A deal can carry a beautiful margin and still be impossible, because margin lives in a ledger and hours live in people. You can't bank 103%.

Run the numbers while the prospect is still a prospect. The version of you on the sales call will agree to anything.

Worked example: the $5,000 retainer

Same studio, same three people. The current book is three retainers: $13,300 a month of revenue against $12,950 of cost. These numbers are a worked example, laid out so you can swap in your own. Now the prospect: $5,000 a month, and the scope needs 40 hours of Alex and 20 of Jordan.

Line by line:


The prospect, a $5,000/mo retainer
Alex, 40 h x $45/h                       $1,800
Jordan, 20 h x $55/h                     $1,100
Cost to serve                            $2,900
Client margin, $5,000 - $2,900           $2,100   (42.00%)

Capacity if you sign as scoped
Alex, 125 + 40 = 165 of 160 h           103.13%   OVER
Jordan, 60 + 20 = 80 of 120 h            66.67%   fine
Casey, unchanged at 115 of 160 h         71.88%   fine

The whole book if you sign
Revenue, $13,300 + $5,000               $18,300
Cost, $12,950 + $2,900                  $15,850
Blended margin                           $2,450   (13.39%)

A 42% client that multiplies the shop's monthly profit by seven, and it still fails. Alex is over capacity before the kickoff call. Signed as scoped, this deal is a resignation letter with a good margin attached.

The same screen shows the fix. Casey has 45 free hours (160 minus 115) at the cheapest rate on the team. Move 10 of the Alex hours to Casey and rerun the what-if:


Alex, 30 h x $45/h                       $1,350
Jordan, 20 h x $55/h                     $1,100
Casey, 10 h x $35/h                        $350
Cost to serve                            $2,800
Client margin                            $2,200   (44.00%)
Alex, 125 + 30 = 155 of 160 h            96.88%
Casey, 115 + 10 = 125 of 160 h           78.13%

Same client, same retainer. Now it fits. Margin even went up $100 a month, because Casey's hours cost less than Alex's. Modeling before committing doesn't just hand you a yes or a no. It hands you the shape of the yes.

The blended-margin trap

Blended margin is total revenue minus total cost, across every client. It's the number the shop actually lives on. It also hides sins.

The example book blends to $350 on $13,300, which is 2.63%. One sick week, one dead laptop, one late payment, and the month goes red.

Inside that blend, the clients look nothing alike. One retainer, Wave Media, loses $150 every month. The invoice gets paid on time, so nobody looks. The healthy clients quietly subsidize it while the owner reads the bank balance and calls things fine.

That's the trap. One bad retainer drags the whole shop, and the blend sits just high enough to hide which one. Client profitability has to be read per client, in dollars and percent, right next to the blend. The 42% prospect lifts this book from 2.63% to 13.39%. Repricing or exiting Wave Media would lift it again.

Three mistakes agency owners make

1. Using pay rates instead of loaded rates. If Jordan's wage is $40 but the seat truly costs $55 with taxes, software, and management time, every priced Jordan hour is $15 optimistic. On this one client that's 20 hours x $15, a $300-a-month error that surfaces at year-end.

2. Planning against the team average. This team averages 68.18% while the deal on the table pushes Alex to 103.13%. Averages don't do the work. People do, one calendar at a time.

3. Never re-running the numbers after the signature. Scope creeps, hours drift, and a retainer priced right in January leaks by June. Nobody prices a client to lose $150 a month; it drifts there. Re-check the whole book monthly, the way you reconcile the bank.

FAQ

How do you calculate agency utilization? Allocated hours divided by capacity hours, per person, per month. Capacity is real availability, not 40 hours times 52 weeks: subtract holidays, admin, and sales time first. Alex with 125 allocated hours against a 160-hour month is 125 / 160, which is 78.13%. Track it per person and leave headroom. A plan that needs anyone at 100% is already broken.

What is a good agency profit margin? After honest loaded rates, I want every client above 30% and the blend above 20%. Those aren't industry statistics, just the floors that keep a small shop alive through a slow quarter. The direction of the math matters more than the target: margin on true cost, per client, next to the blend. A book can blend to 2.63% while every invoice gets paid. Paid isn't profitable.

How do you price a retainer? Estimate hours per person, multiply by loaded rates, then divide by 1 minus your target margin. The prospect above costs $2,900 a month to serve. At a 40% target, $2,900 / 0.60 is $4,833.33, so you quote $5,000 and land at 42.00%. Then check whose capacity those hours come from before the number leaves your mouth. A price that breaks your designer isn't a price. It's a debt.
