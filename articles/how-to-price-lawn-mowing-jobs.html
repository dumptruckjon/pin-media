How to Price Lawn Mowing Jobs (Per Lawn, Not Per Gut Feeling)

Most mowing quotes happen in the truck. You look at the yard, remember what the last one this size paid, and say a number. Some weeks that works. Then October comes, the trailer needs tires, and the season's money doesn't match the season's work.

Pricing lawn care jobs properly is four cost lines, one division, and a check against your route. Here's the whole method, with a worked 3-stop day you can verify line by line.

The cost stack: four lines per lawn

1. Crew labor. People times wage times hours. One person at $22 an hour on a one-hour lawn is $22. Two people for a half hour is still $22. Pay yourself the same wage you'd pay a helper, even solo. If your hour is free, your "profit" is just your paycheck wearing a costume.

2. Equipment. Mowers drink fuel and eat blades, belts, and plugs, and one day the deck rusts through. Carry it as dollars per hour. Add a season of fuel, parts, and repairs to a slice of replacement cost, then divide by the hours you actually mow. The examples below use $12 an hour. Yours might be $8 with one push mower or $20 with a zero-turn and a trailer of two-strokes.

3. Materials. Bags, fertilizer, weed treatment, salt in winter. Most straight mows are zero. Write the line anyway, so the treated jobs don't quietly eat their margin.

4. Drive time. The forgotten one. Between stops the crew is on the clock and the truck is burning fuel, but nobody is paying. Ten minutes of drive at $22 wage plus $12 equipment costs $5.67. That's not rounding error. Across the 33 drive minutes in the route below it's $18.70, and you'll see exactly what it does to the day.

Turn square footage into minutes

Hours = square feet divided by your productivity rate.

Your rate is measurable. Time your next three mows, divide each lawn's square footage by its hours, and average the results. The calculator below ships with 6,000 sqft an hour as its solo default with a mid-size mower. A 21-inch push mower on a fenced lot runs well under that. A zero-turn on open ground runs well over. Gates, slopes, and trim work all drag the rate down, which is why you time your own jobs instead of borrowing a number.

After that, estimating is instant. A 6,000 sqft lawn at 6,000 sqft an hour is 1.00 hour. A 9,000 sqft lawn is 1.50.

The price floor: divide, don't multiply

Price floor = job cost / (1 - target margin)

Say a lawn costs you $52 and you want a 40% margin. Divide by 0.60 and the floor is $86.67. The trap is multiplying by 1.40 instead. That quotes $72.80, and the margin on $72.80 is 28.6%, not 40. The gap is $13.87 of quote, every single mow.

The floor is not the price. It's the number below which the job stops being worth loading the trailer. If your market bears more, charge more.

Why the day's margin is lower than every job's

Job margins get quoted one lawn at a time. Days don't work that way.

On the route below, the three jobs individually run 38.82%, 60.77%, and 39.20% margins. The day runs 37.38%. Lower than every job on it. The gap is drive time: $18.70 of wage and equipment burned between stops, belonging to no invoice. Tight stops keep that gap small. A route scattered across three towns can turn winning jobs into a losing day.

That's also why the floor above excludes drive: the same lawn costs different money depending on where it sits in your route. Count drive at the day level, then fix it with route density or zone pricing, not with a strange line item on one customer's bill.

Worked example: a 3-stop route, to the penny

These are the sample numbers my calculator loads with, so you can check every line. Crew of 1 at $22 an hour, equipment $12 an hour, productivity 6,000 sqft an hour.


Oak Lane Residence: 6,000 sqft -> 1.00 hr
  Labor $22.00 + equipment $12.00 + materials $18.00 = $52.00
  Price $85.00 -> profit $33.00, margin 38.82%

Pine Street Property: timed at 45 min (0.75 hr)
  Labor $16.50 + equipment $9.00 + materials $0 = $25.50
  Price $65.00 -> profit $39.50, margin 60.77%

Maple Grove Estate: 9,000 sqft -> 1.50 hr
  Labor $33.00 + equipment $18.00 + materials $25.00 = $76.00
  Price $125.00 -> profit $49.00, margin 39.20%

Drive between stops: 10 + 8 + 15 = 33 min at $34/hr = $18.70

Revenue                                   $275.00
Costs ($153.50 jobs + $18.70 drive)       $172.20
Profit                                    $102.80
Route margin                               37.38%
Clock time: 3.25 work + 0.55 drive =      3.80 hr
Effective rate: $275.00 / 3.80            $72.37/hr

Three things worth noticing.

Oak Lane misses the 40% target. Its floor is $86.67 and it's priced at $85.00, so it lands at 38.82%. Round the next renewal to $87 and it clears.

Pine Street is the quiet winner: the smallest lawn, no materials, eight minutes away, 60.77%. Small and close beats big and far more often than crews expect.

And the whole day nets $72.37 an hour of clock time, before overhead and taxes. That's the number to watch week over week. Revenue is for bragging; effective rate is for deciding.

Three ways mowing crews underprice

1. Copying the street rate. The guy down the road charges $45, so $45 it is. You can't see his route density, his equipment costs, or whether he's losing money. Copy his number and you can copy his loss. Build your floor first, then look at the street.

2. Paying yourself zero. Skip your own $22 wage and Oak Lane looks like $55 of profit on $30 of cost, a 64.7% margin. It isn't. $22 of that is your hour, unpaid, and a price built that way collapses the day you hire help.

3. Multiplying instead of dividing. Cost times 1.40 is not a 40% margin. It's 28.6%, as shown above. Divide by 1 minus the margin you want, check it once on a phone calculator, and it sticks.

FAQ

How much should I charge for mowing a lawn? There's no national number, and anyone quoting one doesn't know your route. Cost the job: crew wage times hours, equipment per hour, materials. Divide by 1 minus your target margin. A 6,000 sqft lawn at the rates above floors at $86.67 for 40%. Then set a truck minimum, a price below which you don't drop the gate, so tiny lawns still cover their drive.

Should I charge for drive time? Count it, don't invoice it. Customers won't pay a "driving" line, but the cost lands on you either way: $5.67 for a 10-minute hop at the example rates. Recover it by clustering stops on the same day and by pricing far-off one-offs high enough to carry the round trip. If a lawn sits 25 minutes from everything else, its price has to know that.

What about lawn care pricing per square foot? Per-sqft is a decent sanity check and a bad method. Oak Lane works out to about 1.4 cents per sqft. That same rate would misprice Pine Street, which has no materials and barely any drive. Use square footage to estimate hours. Price the hours, the materials, and the margin.
