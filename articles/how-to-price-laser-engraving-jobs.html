Laser Engraving Pricing: A Working Formula

Most laser engraving pricing is a guess. Look at the blank, double it, round to a number that sounds fair, hope. Then a 25-piece order eats a Saturday and the bank account barely moves.

The fix is a cost stack, a two-line formula, and one fully worked batch job. Price your next job with this page and a phone calculator.

The cost stack: five lines, no skipping

Every job has the same five costs. Each line you skip comes straight out of your pocket.

1. Material. Count what the job consumes, not what the receipt says. Blanks are easy: a $6.40 tumbler is $6.40. Sheet goods are sneakier. Say a job cuts 35% of a $12.50 birch sheet. The tidy answer is $4.38, and it's only honest if the other 65% really becomes product later. If that offcut will sit on a shelf for a year, the job used the sheet. Bill more of it.

2. Machine time. Your laser wears out, so treat it like fuel. Divide the purchase price by the hours you expect from it. A $6,000 machine with a 5,000-hour life costs $1.20 an hour, two cents a minute. A $600 diode you'd trust for 2,000 hours runs $0.30 an hour. Your lifespan guess will be wrong, and a wrong small number still beats a confident zero. Tubes, lenses, and belts don't replace themselves.

3. Electricity. Watts times hours times your rate. An 800 W machine running 35 minutes at $0.13 per kWh costs about six cents. Work it out once, add the line, stop thinking about it. It'll be the smallest bar on the chart every time.

4. Labor. The line most people zero out, and often the biggest one. Design time, setup, masking, loading, unloading, inspection, plus the minutes the machine runs, because you shouldn't leave a running laser alone. That's how shop fires start. Machine minutes are labor minutes unless you're truly doing other paid work beside it. Pick an hourly rate and defend it. If you wouldn't engrave a stranger's tumblers for free, stop doing it for your own shop.

5. Failure and waste. Pieces fail. A shifted jig, wrong power, a blank with a bad powder coat. If 1 piece in 20 fails, every good piece has to carry its share of the duds: divide your per-unit cost by 0.95. Divide, don't add. Tacking 5% on top undercounts a little (1 divided by 0.95 is about 1.053), and a failed piece burns your labor and machine time along with the blank.

And overhead, which hides inside the others. Insurance, rent, software, listing fees, card fees: all of it exists even when the laser is off. Two clean ways to carry it: pad your labor rate, or add a flat percentage to every quote. If your platform takes its cut off the top, learn the real percentage and subtract it before calling anything profit.

The formula

Two lines.

True cost per unit = (material + machine time + electricity + labor + consumables) / (1 - failure rate) + setup cost / quantity

Price = true cost / (1 - target margin)

One trap hides in the second line. A 40% margin means profit is 40% of the price, so you divide cost by 0.60. Multiplying by 1.40 feels like the same thing and isn't. On a $12.98 cost, multiplying quotes $18.17 and your real margin lands near 29%. Dividing quotes $21.63 and you get your actual 40.

Worked example: 25 tumblers for the local gym

These are illustrative numbers, laid out so you can swap in your own. A gym orders 25 powder-coated 20 oz tumblers with a one-color logo. Blanks are $6.40 each by the case. Each piece runs 12 minutes on the rotary, and you mask the next blank while the current one engraves, so hands-on time hides inside machine time. Design and setup take 30 minutes once, $12.50 at $25 an hour. The machine is $6,000 with an expected 5,000-hour life. Power is 800 W at $0.13 per kWh. Failure rate 5%. Target margin 40%.


Blank (20 oz powder-coated tumbler)         $6.40
Machine time, 12 min at $1.20/hr            $0.24
Electricity, 0.16 kWh at $0.13/kWh          $0.02
Labor, 12 min at $25/hr                     $5.00
Masking and consumables                     $0.20
Per-unit subtotal                          $11.86

Failure adjustment: $11.86 / 0.95          $12.48
Setup spread: $12.50 / 25 units           + $0.50
True cost per tumbler                      $12.98

Price at 40% margin: $12.98 / 0.60         $21.63
Batch price: 25 x $21.63                  $540.75
Batch cost: 25 x $12.98                   $324.50
Profit on the job                         $216.25

Quote $22 even and your margin is 41%. The job takes about 5.5 attended hours, 25 pieces at 12 minutes plus setup. Revenue $550, profit $225.50, plus the $137.50 of labor you already paid yourself inside the cost stack. That's $363 for 5.5 hours, a $66-an-hour shop rate. Hold that against what "double the blank" would have quoted.

Margin vs the market

The formula sets your floor. The market sets your ceiling.

After you compute $21.63, look at what comparable finished work sells for. If the market price is higher, charge the market price. Nobody hands out medals for being the cheap one, and the extra margin is your cushion for the day a jig slips.

If the market sits below your floor, you have three honest moves. Cut real cost: case-quantity blanks, tighter settings, a batch minimum so setup spreads across more pieces. Sell where buyers value custom work. Or decline the job. A no costs you nothing. And the cheapest listing you found may belong to someone who never ran this math. Match their price and you inherit their loss.

Three ways engravers underprice

1. Pricing from the blank alone. "Double the material" quotes the gym tumbler at $12.80. Its true cost was $12.98. It's underwater by 18 cents before a single platform fee, and it never paid you for a minute of work.

2. Working for free. Unbilled design, "quick" revisions, setup, attendance. Twenty minutes of artwork tweaks is $8.33 at $25 an hour, gone if it isn't a line item. Write revision limits into the quote and bill your minutes.

3. Pretending nothing fails. The failure line looked tiny above, $0.62 a tumbler, but across the batch it's $15.50: the one or two pieces you really will scrap. On sheet jobs, add the offcuts that never get used. Waste is a cost the moment it happens. Price it in before it does.

FAQ

How much should I charge for laser engraving? There's no universal rate, and that's the point: your machine, your labor, and your failure rate make your price. Build the cost stack, divide by 1 minus your target margin, then check the result against the market. For small one-off jobs, set a shop minimum that covers setup plus at least 15 minutes of labor with margin, so tiny work stays worth a yes.

How much does a laser engraver cost to run per hour? Wear plus electricity. The example machine above: $1.20 an hour of depreciation plus about $0.10 of power, call it $1.30 an hour. The machine is cheap to run. The expensive part is the person standing next to it, which is why labor belongs in every quote.

Should I give discounts on bulk orders? Yes, and the formula shows where an honest discount comes from: setup spreading. A single tumbler carries the whole $12.50 setup, so its true cost is $24.98 and a 40% margin prices it at $41.63. In the 25-pack, setup adds $0.50 per piece and the price is $21.63. Same margin both times, roughly half the price, zero charity. Set a minimum margin and never let any quantity tier fall below it.
