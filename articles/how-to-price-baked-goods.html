How to Price Baked Goods: A Working Method

Ask how to price baked goods in any bakers' group and the top answer is "see what the bakery across town charges." So you copy their number, take a custom order, lose a Saturday, and clear less than the ingredient run cost. The fix is a cost stack, batch math, and one honest look at what your market pays.

The cost stack: five lines, every bake

1. Ingredients, at the price you pay. Your store, your brands, this month's receipt, not a national average. The trap is unit math: you buy butter by the pound and use it by the ounce, vanilla by the bottle, use it by the teaspoon. Butter at $4.00 a pound makes 8 oz cost $2.00, and a $4.50 bottle of vanilla holding 8 teaspoons makes one teaspoon about 56 cents. Pantry staples aren't free, just small.

2. Packaging. The box, the board, the ribbon, the sticker. Illustrative: a 60 cent box plus 15 cents of ribbon and label is 75 cents that never appears on a recipe card. Count it per order.

3. Labor, at a wage you'd say out loud. Pick an hourly rate and bill every hands-on minute: mixing, scooping, decorating, boxing, cleanup. The example below uses a modest $15 an hour. Don't bill the hour the oven runs while you fold laundry; do bill everything your hands touch.

4. Overhead. The cottage food permit, insurance, booth fees, oven electricity, the stand mixer that will die someday. It all exists even on weeks you don't bake. Carry it the clean way: divide a month of it by a month of units. $500 across 1,000 baked units is $0.50 a unit. Your numbers will differ; the division is the point.

5. Failure and do-overs. Burnt edges, a cracked cheesecake, the dozen that spread into one tray-sized cookie. If 5% of what you bake never reaches a customer, the pieces that do have to pay for the ones that didn't.

Cost the batch, then the cookie

Recipes happen in batches, sales happen in units. Cost the whole batch, shrink the yield by your waste rate, then divide:

True cost per unit = (batch ingredients + batch labor) / (yield x (1 - waste rate)) + overhead per unit

Price = true cost / (1 - target margin)

The second line hides the classic mistake. A 60% margin means profit is 60% of the price, so you divide cost by 0.40. Multiplying by 1.6 feels like the same thing and isn't: on a $1.24 cost it quotes $1.98, and the real margin at $1.98 is 37.5%, not 60.

Worked example: one batch of chocolate chip cookies

One batch, 24 cookies, 45 minutes of hands-on work at $15 an hour, 5% waste, $500 of monthly overhead across 1,000 monthly units, a 60% margin target. Swap in your own receipts; the shape stays the same.


Butter, 8 oz at $4.00 a pound               $2.00
Flour, 10 oz from a $1.60 bag (2 lb)        $0.50
Granulated sugar, 8 oz                      $0.40
Chocolate chips, 6 oz ($3.00 per 12 oz)     $1.50
Eggs, 2 at $3.60 a dozen                    $0.60
Vanilla, baking soda, salt                  $0.59
Raw ingredient cost per batch               $5.59

Labor, 45 min at $15/hr                    $11.25
Batch subtotal                             $16.84

Waste, 5%: 24 baked, 22.80 sellable
Variable cost per cookie: $16.84 / 22.80    $0.74

Overhead: $500 / 1,000 monthly units        $0.50
True cost per cookie                        $1.24

Price at a 60% margin: $1.24 / 0.40         $3.10
Margin if you sell at $4.25                70.86%
With 8% sales tax, a $4.25 cookie rings     $4.59

The ingredients everyone obsesses over are about a third of the batch subtotal; labor is the other two thirds, at only $15 an hour. A rounding note: recompute the margin from the rounded $1.24 and you get 70.82%; the unrounded $1.2386 under the display is where 70.86% comes from.

Your floor, the market's ceiling

$3.10 is not the price, it's the floor, where your 60% margin starts to dissolve. The ceiling comes from walking the market. If nearby stands sell big single cookies at $4.00 and $4.50 (illustrative), $4.25 isn't greedy, it's the neighborhood, and the example cookie earns 70.86% there. When the market sits above your floor, take the market price; the gap is your cushion for the week butter jumps.

When the market sits below your floor, you have four honest moves: cut real cost (bigger batches spread labor), change the product (minis, mixes, the thing no other stand makes), sell where custom work is valued, or skip that product. Matching a price that loses money just means losing money in company.

The "double the ingredients" trap

The oldest rule in home baking: total the ingredients, double it, done. Against the batch above, ingredients are $5.59, so the rule prices the whole batch at $11.18. Labor alone was $11.25. The rule can't even see the biggest cost on the table.

It gets worse as work gets fancier. A custom cake might carry, illustrative, $18 of ingredients and 5 hours of decorating. Doubling ingredients quotes $36, which after the $18 of ingredients pays $18 for 5 hours of work: $3.60 an hour for the most skilled thing you do. The more labor a bake carries, the harder the rule fails; custom cakes are nearly all labor.

Three pricing mistakes cottage bakers make

1. Copying the bakery across town. Their flour arrives in 50 lb wholesale bags, their oven bakes a dozen trays at once, their rent spreads across thousands of units a month, not eighty. Or they never ran the math either. A competitor's price is decent ceiling data and worthless cost data.

2. Quoting custom work at standard prices. Your everyday dozen and the "can you match this photo" dozen are different products. Custom means design time, a test bake, special supplies, and do-over risk. Price custom from hours, not from the menu.

3. Letting numbers go stale. You costed the recipe last winter and butter has moved since, say 30% (illustrative). Every batch since donated the difference. Recost whenever a receipt surprises you, at least a few times a year on best sellers.

FAQ

How much should I charge for a dozen cookies? There's no universal dozen, only your dozen: batch cost divided by sellable units, times 12, then divided by 1 minus your margin. The example cookie costs $1.24 all-in, so a dozen carries $14.88 of cost, and a 60% margin prices it at $37.20. That's premium single-cookie territory; a plain drop cookie baked 96 at a time spreads labor much thinner and lands far lower. Grocery store dozens are a different product; don't price against them.

Should I charge for my time baking? Yes, at a real wage, even if you love the work. In the example batch, labor at $15 an hour is $11.25, roughly twice the $5.59 ingredient line. Loving the work doesn't make the hour free; charging for it is what keeps the work lovable.

Does cottage food pricing work differently? The method is identical; the constraints are tighter. Permit fees, labels, and inspection costs belong in overhead. Many states cap cottage food revenue per year, which changes strategy: with limited units, margin per unit beats volume, so underpricing hurts twice. Settle sales tax before you print the price card: the example's 8% turns a $4.25 cookie into $4.59 at the register. Rules vary by state, so read yours.
