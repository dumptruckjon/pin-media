How to Manage Custom Cake Orders (Without a Wall of Sticky Notes)

Custom cake orders never arrive in one place. One books through an Instagram message, one through a text, one in person while you hand over a different cake. Each lands wherever it lands: a chat thread, a sticky note on the fridge, your memory. The system holds until the Friday night you scroll three weeks of messages because someone, you're almost sure, said "the 14th." That night is avoidable. Managing custom cake orders comes down to capture, status, and math. Here's a setup for all three.

Capture the whole order when you say yes

An order that lives in a chat thread isn't booked, it's remembered. The moment you say yes, move it to one place, the same place every time, with the same fields every time. That's all a cake order form is. Paper, spreadsheet, or app, it needs:

  • Customer name and the contact they answer. If she ordered by DM but replies to texts, write the phone number.
  • Date and time, pickup or delivery. The most expensive field to get wrong.
  • The deliverables, exactly. Item, quantity, size, flavors, filling, colors, and the inscription spelled the way they spelled it.
  • Allergies and notes. "Nut-free classroom" isn't a detail, it's the order.
  • The money. Quoted price, deposit taken, balance due.
  • A status. More on that next.

Intake takes five minutes. When anything changes later, change the form, not your memory.

Four statuses catch the forgotten order

The forgotten order has a specific shape. It's never the cake you're decorating today; it's the one accepted three weeks out that stayed invisible until it was due tomorrow. Statuses exist to make far-off orders visible:

  • Pending. Booked, nothing started. Orders sit here longest, and this is where forgetting happens.
  • In progress. Supplies bought, something in motion.
  • Ready. Boxed and waiting for handoff.
  • Delivered. Handed over, paid in full.

Keep every order in exactly one of the four, sort by date, and read the list twice a week. Then add the rule that does the heavy lifting: anything due inside 72 hours gets flagged loud, started or not. Color it, star it, move it to the top of the page. A pending order inside that window is Tuesday's emergency, not Friday's disaster. The flag outranks the statuses.

Know it pays before you say yes

The best time to learn an order doesn't pay is before you accept it. So run the numbers while the quote is still a draft: items and quantities, your hours at your rate, ingredients. Thin margin? Raise the quote or pass while it costs you nothing. Building the price itself is its own topic (ingredient math, overhead, waste, target margins); I walked through it in the pricing guide. This article only argues about timing. Quote from numbers, then accept.

Worked example: two dozen cupcakes

Here's the intake math on one order. A customer asks for two dozen vanilla cupcakes at $3.50 each. You expect 90 minutes of work and pay yourself $22 an hour:


Revenue: 24 x $3.50                       $84.00
Labor: 90 minutes at $22 an hour          $33.00
Cost so far (labor only)                  $33.00
Gross profit so far                       $51.00
Margin so far: $51.00 / $84.00             60.7%

That 60.7% flatters you because no ingredients have landed yet. Add the cupcake recipe's costs (illustrative: $12.60 of ingredients and boxes for the batch) and the picture completes:


Total cost: $33.00 + $12.60               $45.60
Gross profit: $84.00 - $45.60             $38.40
Margin: $38.40 / $84.00                    45.7%

$38.40 for a Saturday morning is a fine order. Say yes. The point isn't this one answer. It's that the answer showed up before you replied, and a money-losing order would have surfaced the same way, as a margin you could refuse.

One prep sheet for the whole Saturday

Custom cake orders cluster on weekends, and working them one at a time wastes the overlap. Say Saturday holds the cupcakes above, a 6-inch smash cake, and four dozen sugar cookies. Worked separately, that's three rounds of creaming butter and three sinks of dishes. Worse, you find out mid-afternoon that order three needed the vanilla you finished at noon.

So before the day starts, sum the day. One production sheet: every ingredient totaled across every Saturday order, every component listed once. Cream butter once. Make one big batch of buttercream and tint from a single base. Batching is the closest thing this work has to free labor, and a summed sheet is how you see the overlap while there's still time to use it.

Keep a customer ledger

Repeat clients are the business. The mom who ordered the first-birthday smash cake needs a second-birthday cake, then a classroom dozen every June. Keep a page per customer: every order, every date, what they paid. Add the notes that make the next order easy: "loved the lemon filling," "pickup always runs late."

When she texts next March, you'll know it's her fourth order and that the classroom is nut-free before she retypes it. A supermarket bakery can't offer that. The ledger is also a working list: your top customers by lifetime spend are the first to hear when holiday slots open.

Three order-management mistakes

1. The order stays where it landed. A booking that lives in an Instagram thread is a rumor with a date. Take orders in five apps if you like, but manage them in one place, moved there within minutes of the yes.

2. No deposit, no booking. An excited "yes!!" costs the customer nothing. A no-show costs you ingredients, hours, and the order you turned away to fit it. Until a deposit lands, it's interest, not an order. Interest doesn't get a slot.

3. Booking by empty calendar instead of available hours. Three fine orders can share a Saturday and still sink it. At 5, 4, and 5 hours of hands-on work (illustrative), that blank square is holding 14 hours. Track booked hours per day and check the total before accepting. Your hands are the capacity, not the calendar.

FAQ

How do I take custom cake orders? Let the conversation start anywhere, then move it straight onto your cake order form: contact, date and time, exact deliverables, allergies, price, deposit, status. Confirm back in writing with the total, the deposit amount, and the pickup time. It's booked when the form is filled and the deposit is paid, not when the chat gets a heart.

Should I take a deposit for cakes? Yes, for anything custom. A deposit filters ghost orders before you shop for them. It also makes the booking mutual: both sides now lose something if the date falls through. A 50% deposit to hold the date, balance at pickup, is a clean policy. Put it on the order form so it never needs debating in chat.

How far in advance should cake orders be placed? Set a lead-time rule and post it wherever people order. For a home bakery, 1-2 weeks for standard custom work and 3-4 weeks for tiered or holiday-week cakes is workable. The exact number matters less than enforcing one; inside the window, quote a rush fee or decline. Early orders also batch better, because a fuller Saturday sheet has more overlap to save.
